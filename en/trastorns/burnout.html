Stress and work

🔥 Burnout (occupational exhaustion)

Exhaustion caused by chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed.

Information, not a diagnosisThis page is for information only. Only a professional can assess your situation.

What it is

Burnout, or occupational burn-out syndrome, is what can appear when stress at work lasts a long time and is not resolved. The World Health Organization (WHO) describes it in ICD-11 as an occupational phenomenon, not as a disease or a mental disorder, and relates it only to the work context. It is not the same as a hard week: the exhaustion persists and changes how you experience work. The symptoms are similar to those of depression or anxiety and can overlap with them, which is why it is worth having a professional assess it.

Main source: WHO: Burn-out an "occupational phenomenon": International Classification of Diseases (in English)

Common symptoms

  • Exhaustion and a feeling of having no energy, even after resting
  • Mental distance from work: cynicism, negativity or loss of motivation
  • Feeling less effective, like you are getting nowhere or doing everything wrong
  • Difficulty concentrating, remembering or making decisions
  • Irritability or emotions that overflow easily
  • Sleep problems, headaches, muscle tension or digestive discomfort
  • A tiredness that does not improve at the weekend or on holiday

Myths and facts

Myth: Burnout happens to weak people or people who can’t manage their time.
Reality: The WHO links it to chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed, and Spain’s National Institute for Safety and Health at Work (INSST) explains that it arises mainly from harmful work organisation. It is not a matter of character.
Myth: A holiday will fix it.
Reality: Rest helps, but if the causes continue (excessive workload, lack of control, little recognition, conflicts) the exhaustion comes back. Often something at work needs to change too.
Myth: It is a medical diagnosis.
Reality: In ICD-11 it is an “occupational phenomenon”, not a disease. A professional can assess whether there is also depression, anxiety or another problem that can be treated.

When to see a professional

If the exhaustion lasts for weeks, affects your work and personal life, you sleep badly or feel hopeless, talk to your family doctor or a psychologist. You can also contact your company’s occupational risk prevention service. If you have thoughts of harming yourself or not wanting to live, call 024 or 112 now.

What usually helps

Because it is not a medical diagnosis, there is no single treatment: it depends on each person and each job. Usually a combination is used: rest, changes at work (workload, limits, schedules, support), psychological help to manage stress and set limits, and getting back to activities that do you good. If depression or anxiety are also present, they are treated with the usual approaches. Talking to a doctor about adjustments at work or sick leave is a decision for a healthcare professional.

Tips for family and friends

  • Listen without saying “you just need to rest” or “it’s not that bad”.
  • Ask how you can help: taking over some household tasks for a while can make a big difference.
  • Encourage them to see a doctor or a psychologist, especially if they sleep very badly, withdraw from others or talk about seeing no way out.
  • Respect that they may need to change schedule or job, and that this is not an easy decision.

Free tools for burnout

All of them are free and do not ask you for an account. Your data is stored only on your device.

On this website

  • To lower your activation: guided breathing, muscle relaxation and 5-4-3-2-1 grounding.
  • To regain energy: behavioural activation (small activities for pleasure and mastery) and sleep diary.
  • To see how you feel: mood log and thought diary.
  • To decide on a first step: My plan, with five questions and a suggestion of small steps.
  • To measure exhaustion: the CBI questionnaire (Copenhagen Burnout Inventory), in the public domain and indicative only.
  • To rule out other causes: the PHQ-9 and GAD-7 screenings do not measure burnout, but they help show whether there are symptoms of depression or anxiety.

From official sources

  • WHO guide “Doing What Matters in Times of Stress”: short techniques for managing stress, with illustrations. A Spanish version adapted for Latin America is also available.
  • INSST: occupational burnout syndrome (in Spanish): official information and links to technical notes on prevention and assessment.
  • WHO: burn-out as an occupational phenomenon in ICD-11.
